## Why this matters

Your team is probably already using AI, with or without guidance. A short, clear policy beats an unwritten one: people know what is allowed, and you reduce the risk of sensitive data landing in the wrong place. This guide works with Claude, Grok Bot, ChatGPT, or any other tool.

## What you’ll need

- A list of the AI tools people already use
- A sense of the data your business handles (client, financial, employee, health, payment)
- Anyone who handles legal, HR, or IT questions for your company
- A reviewer who can say whether it fits your industry’s obligations

## Step-by-step

### 1. Take stock

Ask the team which tools they use and for what. Do this without blame; you want honest answers.

### 2. Sort your data into three tiers

- **Never enter:** passwords, payment card numbers, regulated or highly sensitive personal data, and anything covered by a confidentiality agreement that forbids it
- **Only in approved tools:** client documents, financials, internal strategy
- **Fine anywhere:** public information, generic drafting, brainstorming

### 3. Draft the policy with an AI assistant

```
Draft a one-page AI acceptable-use policy for [COMPANY], a [INDUSTRY] business with [EMPLOYEE COUNT] staff.

Tools currently approved: [TOOL LIST AND PLAN TYPES]
Data tiers: [PASTE YOUR THREE TIERS]
Obligations we know about: [CONTRACTS, INDUSTRY RULES]

Include: purpose, approved tools, data rules, human review before external use, disclosure expectations, how to report a mistake, and who to ask.
Use plain language, under 500 words. List any assumptions you made and any questions I should answer.
```

### 4. Get a real review

Have your IT, HR, or legal contact review the draft. The AI draft is a starting point, not legal advice.

### 5. Roll it out

Share it in a 15-minute team talk, with two real examples of “OK” and “not OK.” Put it where people will find it and set a review date, such as every six months.

> **Tip:** Include a “not sure? ask [NAME]” line. People follow rules more when asking is easy.

> **Watch out:** A policy nobody reads is worse than none. Keep it to one page and use examples.

## Tips

- Reference your actual tools and plans so the rules are concrete.
- Add a short incident process: tell someone quickly, no blame.
- Revisit the policy when tools or contracts change.

## Common pitfalls

- Banning everything, which pushes use underground
- Copying a template without adapting it to your data
- No named owner for questions

## Team Safety & Privacy Note

- Never put credentials in policy examples; point to your password manager instead.
- Obligations differ by industry and location; confirm with a qualified advisor.
- Do not include real client or employee information in training examples.
